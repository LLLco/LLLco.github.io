THE GAME
Screenshot of late gameplay, with a fairly tall tower.
Tower of Genn tasks the player with a simple gameplay loop: build a tower! For the longest time as a game designer I've had a fascination with towers as backdrops for gameplay- they carry within them such infinite vectors of design.

Inspired by games such as Dorfromantik, Tetris, and Jenga, players use tetracubes and other 3d shapes comprised of 'Special Blocks' to assemble their tower. The loop is tightly moderated by the player's need to keep their 'Stockpile' up, being drained for each piece placed.

Players have multiple inflows in which they might gain stockpile, such as through the bonus bar, or quests! Quests form an important backbones of the balancing as a primary inflow. They exist under the concept of "Put it on the board," ie, the quests themselves are blocks on the tower, that have special conditions that must be met.

BLOCKS
Editor view of all the current blocks. Shout-out to our 3d artist!
Blocks make up the fundamental gameplay of Tower of Genn. Players use blocks to essentially construct the level themselves, facing choices that will cascade into different decisions overtime. If they aren't careful, they might build themselves into a corner!

Blocks are designed such that they will carry immersive properties, by having property tags that carry within them rules. Each block contains a unique function that works within these tags, such as the capability of the 'Lava Block' to burn blocks tagged with 'Flammable.'

All this comes together to create a strategic immersive simulation of sorts, inspired by my time with games such as Breath of the Wild and Deus Ex!

QUESTS
Here the player has two active quests on their tower,
designated by the orange icons.
Quests, being such an inflow for the primary gameplay driving resource (stockpile), were very important to get right. Originally, they existed as side objectives that could be completed anywhere, but that wasn't very fun. It lacked a certain "presence" in a game where you build the experience you play.

That's when quest blocks became a thing! I was inspired by the design concept of "put it on the board," that I first heard in the context of Starcraft. Using some inspiration from Dorfromantik, I redesigned the quests to be icons attached to a specific block, that demanded a specific localized action. This was VERY well recieved by playtesters, and is frequently the most desired for content expansions.
